# Chapter 1: Marney’s Beanery

Marney’s Beanery was a sports bar in precisely the sense that a Class-4 biohazard suit was a backless wedding gown: it was technically airtight, completely devoid of charm, and guaranteed to suffocate anyone foolish enough to seek romance inside it. The City had officially classified it as an athletic establishment because no one inside was permitted to perform an act of athleticism, which suited the patrons immensely. They were all students and postdocs, and nothing ruined their mood faster than the sight of a fit, good-looking guy doing sport. 

Dozens of television screens hung from the ceiling like digital gravestones above rows of bottles that held not spirits but glycerol and potassium acetate, in which Marney had obsessively preserved rare pathology specimens salvaged from foreclosed laboratories over the decades. A patron could sit on a stool for a month, staring at the screens, without ever learning what a quarterback did or who was currently failing to do it. Instead of massive, sweating men colliding for regional pride, the screens broadcast the cold, indifferent ballet of bioluminescent jellyfish; a looping microscopic time-lapse of rat hippocampal neurons failing to form a synapse; and slow-motion footage of an African cheetah sprinting with magnificent, undeniable athletic perfection. Everyone in the bar ignored the cheetah, because it was legally impossible to persuade an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee to permit research on an endangered species in California.

The ambient roar of the tavern was entirely devoid of honest, drunken, macho tribalism. In its place hung a venomous, whispered crossfire of underpaid postdocs disputing one another’s methods. Beneath the hissing lay the frantic whirring of cooling fans in decade-old laptops: machines bought by weeping, middle-class parents who had ecstatically daydreamed of Nobel Prize acceptance speeches the day their child’s admission letter arrived, only for owner and machine alike to end up exhausted by a hegemony of free-porn-tube malware. Eight years later, they were running ill-tempered simulations written hastily at 3:00 AM by a computationally inept student who believed “eigenspace” was a municipal parking garage in suburban Frankfurt.

Axel, a seventh-year PhD student, slouched at the bar, choking down a synth-brew that tasted remarkably like warm urine, which was a great comfort to him, because cold urine would have tasted significantly worse. Axel was a reasonable man who found profound relief in catastrophes that were slightly less catastrophic than other catastrophes. Beside his glass lay a saucer of heterogeneously salted peanuts that were either too salty to eat or not salty enough to taste, so that by eating a carefully calculated cluster of them he achieved a flawless state of total deprivation. He was drowning in draft 0.165 of his PhD thesis, working with the furious, dedicated inefficiency of a man who knew that doing nothing would take far too long. Magnificent academic progress was being made: he was replacing every instance of *regulation* with *modulation*. Modulation was clearly superior, because it sounded like he knew what he was doing, whereas *regulation* merely shouted that the author had read his first neuroscience paper yesterday afternoon. Before it became *regulation*, it had been *tuning*, which had replaced *gating*, which had replaced *biasing* on Tuesday, which had replaced three frantic question marks at 4:00 AM, which had originally been *reconfiguration*, back before he had needed to look up “academic integrity” in a dictionary. In the morning, he intended to change it all back to *regulation*, which would allow him to feel that he had accomplished a great deal of vital work, because if he had not changed it to *modulation* first, he never would have had anything to undo.

In a dark booth near the restrooms, kid sobbing in the dark booth behind Axel’s stool. She was weeping with the fierce, regulated discipline of a ten-year-old girl who had just deduced that Santa Claus was not only real, but legally restrained from coming within one hundred yards of any municipal playground. Her Principal Investigator had just dropped by to deliver the marvelous news, and the marvelous news was a total catastrophe. The ultraviolet blip she had extracted from the microtubules of monkey brains—the magnificent, revolutionary anomaly she had cataloged, correlated, and worshiped for four grueling years—was not quantum entanglement. It was not macroscopic superradiance, either, nor was it the luminous seat of primate consciousness. It was the lab Mr. Coffee, which possessed a frayed ground wire and a punctual habit of sparking violently every time someone brewed a pot of French roast, which happened to occur precisely three minutes before she initiated every trial, because she liked having her coffee ready the exact second an experiment concluded. It was a splendid breakthrough, her Principal Investigator explained with immense satisfaction, because it meant she was finally finished. She didn't have to analyze the data anymore, which was an enormous relief, because having no data left to analyze meant she was completely free to do nothing, which was precisely what she had been doing for the last four years without knowing it.

Two tables over, a gaunt third-year PhD candidate was torturing a dataset on a glowing monitor while a Mercenary Postdoc hovered over his shoulder like a medieval inquisitor.

“It’s at zero-point-zero-six-two,” the student whimpered, his fingers trembling over the trackpad. “It refuses to cross the threshold. It’s statistically insolent.”

“Break its legs,” the postdoc instructed coldly, taking a sip of tepid gin. “Log-transform the reaction times, drop the Tuesday trials on the grounds of barometric interference, and run a one-tailed test with a Bonferroni correction applied in reverse. We are not leaving this booth until that p-value confesses to zero-point-zero-four-nine.”

Near the radiator sat a permanent fixture of the tavern’s furniture: a cluster of seven undergraduates who existed in a state of rigid, binary deadlock. They never ordered drinks, they never finished their homework, and they were physically incapable of discussing anything that could not be reduced to a blood feud between two irreconcilable absolutes. On Tuesdays it was Linux versus Mac; on Thursdays it was Musk’s imminent AI utopia versus Altman’s inevitable apocalypse; tonight the table was violently deadlocked over whether David Lynch’s dream-logic was objectively superior to Christopher Nolan’s non-linear exposition. That screaming match ran in parallel with an adjacent argument, at the same table, comparing the ontological validity of Einstein’s local realism with the Copenhagen interpretation, which itself kept devolving into a fistfight over Beyoncé versus Taylor Swift. Whenever one of the undergraduates graduated or dropped out, another identical nineteen-year-old in the exact same flannel shirt immediately materialized to defend iOS against Android to the death.

Presiding over the center of the room like a decaying Hellenistic monument was Papa—short for Dr. Panagiotis Papagiannopoulos—a fifty-two-year-old Greek eternal postdoc who had arrived on a temporary exchange visa during the Clinton administration and had simply refused to stop filing extensions. His mastery of human knowledge spanned the breadth of the Pacific Ocean and achieved a uniform depth of roughly one inch. He read voraciously across continental philosophy, neuroeconomics, quantum thermodynamics, non-Euclidean textile geometry, embroidery, and institutional history, distilling the titles and abstracts of thousands of papers into terrifyingly plausible lectures that he inflicted on cornered first-year rotation students, who didn’t yet know how to fake a pager alert to escape him.

He currently had three fresh-faced, first-week doctoral students pinned against the radiator, holding them hostage with an unlit rolled cigarette and the gravitas of a man who had survived four department chairs.

“Tell me,” Papa rumbled, his voice a rich, gravelly baritone soaked in thirty years of cheap espresso and institutional betrayal. “What is the definition of doing the exact same thing over and over again, and expecting a different result?”

One of the first-years—a tragically eager kid still wearing his orientation lanyard—perked up, desperate for extra credit even in a tavern. “Oh! I know this one! Einstein said it. It’s—”

“Wrong!” Papa barked, slicing the air with two nicotine-stained fingers before the boy could arrive at the word *insanity*. “First of all, Albert Einstein never said that. It was misattributed to him by a twelve-step recovery pamphlet in the nineteen-eighties, because Americans will believe any platitude so long as you glue a mustache and a German accent to it. Second, the pamphlet was wrong. Doing the exact same thing over and over again while praying to God for a different result is not insanity. It is the scientific method.”

The three first-years blinked in unison, their smiles evaporating.

“Think about it!” Papa leaned in, lowering his voice to the confidential register of a senior investigator sharing classified lab protocols. “If you run an experiment once and get a result, what do they call it? An anecdote. Unpublishable garbage! So what do they force you to do? They force you to repeat the identical protocol five hundred times—n equals five hundred! Under strictly controlled, invariant conditions, praying with every single trial that the variance shifts just enough to give you a different statistical outcome! Why? Because of the Copenhagen interpretation!”

The kid in the lanyard swallowed hard. “The… quantum mechanics thing?”

“The observer effect!” Papa tapped his temple knowingly. “In quantum physics, the act of measurement collapses the wave function. In modern academia, the act of funding collapses the literature gap! Before you look at your data, your hypothesis exists in a pristine quantum superposition of both True and False. The moment you observe it, it collapses into Null, because nature hates you. Unless—” Papa raised a dramatic finger toward the ceiling “—you introduce a macroeconomic observer with a heavy enough gravitational field. Namely, the National Institutes of Health.”

He took a slow drag from his unlit cigarette, letting the horror sink in.

“You children think the NIH and the NSF are deep-pocketed science sugar daddies?” Papa chuckled, a dark, hollow sound. “Look at the neuroeconomics of the R01 study section. It is a closed thermodynamic loop. The NSF funds the physicists to prove that reality is probabilistic, while the NIH pays the neuroscientists thirty billion dollars a year to repeat failed behavioral assays until a twenty-four-year-old graduate student, hallucinating from sleep deprivation, accidentally forces a p-value across an arbitrary threshold invented by an English eugenics enthusiast in 1925! You are not discovering biology, my young friends. You are human particle accelerators! They lock you in a basement to bang the same two variables together ten thousand times until your collective cortisol levels physically warp the local probability distribution and manufacture a false positive! That is what a grant renewal is! It is state-sponsored wormhole tunneling through a wall of negative results!”

The youngest rotation student looked down at her hands, suddenly terrified that her lab notebook was an instrument of federal psychological warfare. “So… what happens when the grant runs out?” she whispered.

“Decoherence!” Papa said solemnly, placing a heavy, paternal hand on her shoulder. “Your preliminary data reverts to noise, your advisor pretendssnever met you, and you wake up at forty-five in a booth in West Los Angeles, explaining stochastic terrorism to children who still think they’re going to cure Alzheimer’s.”

From behind the counter, where he had been carefully topping off a jar containing a two-headed fetal calf with fresh potassium acetate, Marney sighed heavily. He rapped a scarred knuckle on the mahogany to catch a passing waitress’s attention, then pointed at Papa.

“Hey, Papa!” Marney called across the gloom, his voice cutting cleanly through the first-years’ existential dread. “Don’t scare the children. Have a complimentary brew, my friend—your throat gets dry when you’re unifying physics.”

Papa paused, his tragic-prophet pose melting instantly. He inclined his head toward the bar, accepted the sweating cold pint from the waitress as though it were a MacArthur Genius Grant, and raised the glass to the three paralyzed students. “Drink up, kids,” he advised cheerfully, wiping a mustache of chemical foam from his lip. “The first three years are the hardest. After the seventh, the Stockholm syndrome starts to feel like tenure.”

Behind the bar, Marney returned to wiping down the taps with the serene, unreadable expression of a deity tending a terrarium of particularly dim-witted beetles. Nobody at the university knew what Marney had actually studied for his undergraduate degree, or which department had granted his PhD, largely because he could effortlessly hijack any conversation in the room and cite the foundational literature of any discipline by author, journal, and year of publication without looking up from the dish pit. He had once corrected a visiting Nobel laureate’s organic chemistry mechanism while simultaneously explaining to a weeping comparative literature student why her reading of Derrida was derivative of a 1974 footnote in *Telos*.

According to departmental folklore, Marney had been a once-in-a-century academic supernova: a superstar student across five incompatible disciplines and a legendary postdoctoral fellow who had simultaneously held a dozen tenure-track offers, from the Ivy League to Max Planck. And then, in an act of supreme intellectual clarity that baffled the scientific community, he had turned every single one of them down to buy a neighborhood strip club directly across the street from both the university’s main gates and the adjoining St. Jude’s Episcopal Church. 

In a whirlwind forty-eight-hour campaign of pure, weaponized charisma, he lobbied every billionaire donor, terrified trustee, and guilt-ridden bishop on the West Coast, pooling an emergency moral-preservation endowment of staggering proportions. He bought the building, kept the original staff on the payroll at full university benefits, and immediately rebranded the establishment as Marney’s Beanery—thereby trading a house of naked women who made men feel inadequate for a house of clothed academics who made everyone feel suicidal.

For over a decade, Marney had been quietly skimming the interest off the leftover endowment to subsidize the bar’s operations heavily. To minimize overhead and eliminate the cognitive load of choice for his mentally exhausted patrons, he offered a strict, single-item menu: a heavy ceramic bowl of chili and beans, priced at two dollars and forty-five cents and nutritionally engineered to sustain human life through a six-month grant-writing cycle. To wash it down, he brewed his own synthetic house lager in the basement—a lukewarm, urine-colored concoction that tasted like despair, but which Marney had meticulously buffered with thiamine, magnesium, and proprietary neuroprotective agents, ensuring that no matter how aggressively a graduate student tried to drink into oblivion, the alcohol was scientifically tempered so that not a single brain cell would actually die. It was the ultimate academic cruelty: at Marney’s, you couldn’t even damage the organ that was ruining your life.

The clientele of Marney’s consisted of two demographics: those who were desperately trying to climb into the ivory tower of science, and those who had already been thrown out of its upper-story windows. Both groups were united in the absolute, unshakable conviction that the other had made the worst mistake.

Inside this academic purgatory, money was entirely useless because nobody had any. Transactions were conducted strictly in degrading favors, bootleg ethanol, house chili, synthetic nutrients designed to cure diseases no one had ever caught, and expertise. Expertise was a brilliant commodity, utterly worthless to anyone who possessed it and absolutely indispensable to anyone who did not. A man who had it could do nothing with it, and a man who lacked it could do nothing without it, which made it the most fiercely contested currency in the room, outvalued only by unsolicited, unoffered, strictly noncommittal acts of perversion that were never quite carried out, leaving everyone involved feeling terribly unaccomplished.

Graduate students carrying terminally unreadable theses and towering heaps of pure electronic static, which they dignified with the name of datasets, slipped through the shadows to purchase salvation from disgraced Doctors of Philosophy like Morteza, a man of science who had discovered that science was entirely negotiable. He sat slumped beneath a buzzing neon pear that possessed the distinct, unmistakable, and utterly shameless contours of a human buttock viewed from the posterolateral angle, waiting patiently to perform small mathematical miracles for anyone whose desperation exceeded their shame. It was an airtight transaction: Morteza performed the statistical miracles required to keep the university from discovering that they had learned nothing, and in return the students paid him with fourth authorship on papers nobody would ever read, and a week’s worth of Marney’s biotin-fortified chili and beans.

Morteza, whom everyone in the department fondly called Morty despite his best efforts to teach them to say his name correctly, was having a profoundly slow evening. Beside him sat Axel, laboring furiously at replacing words to demonstrate that he was hard at work editing his thesis. Axel had made a solemn vow never to accept Morty’s suggestions under any circumstances. This was not because they were rarely correct or helpful, but because accepting one would prove the thesis was flawed, and if the thesis was flawed, Axel would have to fix it, which was entirely out of the question since he was already far too busy pretending to fix it.

Morty took a long, diplomatic sip of coolant and, with all the creeping stealth of an advancing glacier, began inching his body toward Axel’s screen. “Classic meta-null scenario, kid. Seen it a million times,” he rasped. “Back in my postdoc days, I actually Bayesploded my way out of a very similar temporal causality paradox.”

Axel blinked, his fingers hovering over the keyboard. “You… you what?”

“Bayesplosion,” Morty repeated, executing a grand, drunken sweep of his hand that nearly liberated a jar of pickled sheep brains from the adjacent shelf. “You generate so many gnarly probabilistic models, so many alternative hypotheses, that the reviewers simply give up trying to figure it out.”

“How is that even possible?” Axel asked, genuinely confused, which was his default epistemological state.

Morty leaned forward, delighted, having waited all evening for that exact question. “It creates an induced impostor syndrome. If a paper makes sense, a reviewer will tear it to shreds, because they understand it well enough to find the flaws. If it makes no sense at all, they reject it because you’re an idiot. The trick is to make it *almost* make sense, at a level of mathematical absurdity so needlessly aggressive that the reviewers instantly assume there is something fundamental they are missing. They panic. Nobody wants to admit to the editor that they can’t read a fourth-order tensor. So they declare you a pioneer just to make the reading stop. They accept your manuscript out of sheer statistical exhaustion. Technically, it’s just a Type I error, but hey—it’s stat-sig for you. Okay, let me show you …”

Before Axel could figure out how to incorporate Morty’s dubious wisdom into his understanding of the scientific method, Morty turned the laptop toward himself and began typing. Axel was desperately trying to pry Morty’s hands off the keyboard, as though they were hot potatoes fresh from a campfire, when a figure in cerulean surgical scrubs—radiating the brisk, institutional stench of ozone and hospital disinfectant—strode out of the dimness with immense, terrifying purpose and sat down directly beside him. She smiled with all the warm, natural spontaneity of an SNL host squinting at a cue card held slightly too far away.

“Axel! What a coincidence. It’s been so long since I’ve seen you—I was just thinking about you.”

Axel, whose nervous system was conditioned to treat any attention from a female peer as a miraculous statistical anomaly, woke up instantly. He couldn’t stop a wide, thoroughly helpless smile from spreading across his face. “What?!”

Azie slid closer, continuing enthusiastically, as if she had been waiting purely for that smile to confirm that his motor cortex was online. “Professor Lazlo has a rogue Parkinson’s patient out in Vegas, and I thought to myself: who is better for it than you?”

Axel’s smile flickered. “Wait—what? Morty, would you stop typing in my thesis?” He pulled the laptop back toward himself.

“A total DBS firmware meltdown,” Azie said briskly. “Apparently, the guy cashed out his pension, tweaked his own program, eloped with a hooker, and now his implant is making him generate pure-signal predictions. He’s winning blackjack faster than the eye can see and causing major chaos on the floor, until his motor loop locked up, and he froze solid in the final round of a poker tournament.”

Axel perked up, a glint of reluctant curiosity cutting through his bleary eyes. “Ah. And what, exactly, does that have to do with us? … Me?”

Azie ignored Morty entirely, focusing her laser-like clinical gaze solely on Axel. “We need to get out to the Bellagio in Vegas to reprogram the shakie.”

Axel offered a thin, sarcastic smile. “Since when does the Bellwether University pays out-of-state house visits?”

“Since it’s one of the Clinic’s patients,” Azie hissed. “Lazlo doesn’t want a massive PR drama right before the grand opening of his new center. It’s a quick trip to Vegas, Axel. A quick re-pro, one night of free booze and lotto, and a giant, buttery-tender masterpiece of a steak on Lazlo.”

Axel recoiled, as if the desert heat of Nevada had already enveloped him. “Vegas? Azie, I can’t. I’m… I’m too depressed to travel. My entire emotional connectome is currently experiencing a glial drift into a local minimum. I just can’t.”

Azie narrowed her eyes, her surgical training leaving zero room for psychiatric abstractions. “Depression is a down-regulation of affective valence, Axel, not a physical impediment to locomotion. We’re talking about a neural intervention, not a therapy session.”

“I’m also agoraphobic!” Axel blurted, clutching his pint glass like a talisman. “The wide-open spaces of the desert… and acrophobic—I can’t fly at all, I can’t even be in a cabin, I’m severely claustrophobic…” Azie narrowed her eyes until they seemed about to emit a laser beam. “I can’t do this job, I’m bad at it, I’m… I’m… I’m an alcoholic!”

Azie dipped her finger in his pint and tasted it. “By drinking this? My grandma’s armpit has more alcohol in it. Axel! Please, please, please! I need this. I’m femur-deep in trouble, and that bastard Lazlo sits on my Residency Review Committee. If he tanks my surgical sign-off, my career is dead on the table. I need to pull this off. I’ll do anything you say. I’ll do your laundry for a month.”

Axel shuddered violently. “I won’t let your fingers—which have touched every single STD-juju-dripping hoohaa in Los Angeles—even come close to my delicates.”

“I’ll let you suckle my nipples.”

“Ew, Azie! Stop offering biohazard proposals!”

Morty placed his palms on the table and stood up with sudden, heroic dignity. “Okay, I’ll do it.”

“Shut up, Morty!” Azie and Axel barked in unison.

Azie leaned across the table, lowering her voice. “You need this too, Axel. You pull Lazlo out of this fire, and he has the clout to get you a second-paper exemption. You won’t need another publication to graduate.”

“I won’t need an exemption,” Axel said, waving a hand dismissively toward his screen. “This round I’ll convince the third reviewer. Bada-bing, bada-boom, and I’m graduated.”

Azie stared at him with the solemn, pitying silence she usually reserved for patients whose brain scans looked like dropped lasagnas. “Axel! You know it’s not going to happen. You didn’t get *a* third reviewer.” She paused, letting the fatal words hang in the stale air of Marney’s. “You got *the* Third Reviewer.”

Axel’s gaze dropped slowly back to his laptop, where the word *modulation* blinked in lonely terror. Seeing his spirit fracture, Azie was already pulling out her phone, her voice dropping to an Elizabeth Holmes–style seductive octave. “We won’t fly. Why would we? We’ll go in the university’s optimal transit van, a sealed, climate-controlled, Wi-Fi-enabled mobile lab, literally an Edison jail…”

“Faraday cage! Jesus, Azie.”

“Yes! You love Faraday. Don’t you? Imagine being alone with Faraday in a cage, directly VPN’d to the campus data center. You’ll barely know you’ve left the server room. A huge van—even an acromegalic giant won’t feel claustrophobic in it. It’s fun!”

Axel sighed, his shoulders collapsing. “Fine!”

“Have I, recently, told you how much I love you?” she announced, already turning and marching away from the table while barking instructions into her phone.

“No, you haven’t… ever,” Axel murmured to the empty air.

With that, Azie pushed through the heavy oak door and was gone. Axel closed his laptop, leaving half his thesis regulated and the other half modulated. He took one last, mournful look at his warm, yellow beer, in which Azie had baptized her finger moments before; at the jellyfish drifting across the screens; at Papa terrorizing the first-years; and at the comforting, hopeless cacophony of Marney’s Beanery. He did not know that within a month he would miss all of it so much that he would give anything to have it back.
