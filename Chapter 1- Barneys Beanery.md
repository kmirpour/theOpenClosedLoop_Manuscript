Barney’s Beanery was a sports bar in precisely the same sense that a Class-4 biohazard suit was a backless wedding gown: it was technically airtight, completely devoid of charm, and guaranteed to suffocate anyone foolish enough to seek romance inside it. It had been officially classified as an athletic establishment by the City because no one inside was allowed to ever do an act of athleticism, which suited the patrons who where all students and postdoc immensely, as nothing ruined their mood faster than seeing a fit, physically capable, good looking person doing sport.

It had dozens of television screens suspended like digital gravestones above the liquor bottles and walls, yet a patron could sit cogitate on a stool for a month without ever discovering what a quarterback did or who was currently failing to do it. Instead of massive, sweating men colliding for points, the monitors broadcasted the cold, indifferent ballet of bioluminescent jellyfish, a looping microscopic time-lapse of rat neurons failing to communicate, and slow-motion footage of an African cheetah sprinting with magnificent, undeniable athletic perfection that everyone completely ignored because it was of no statistical relevance to anybody's doctoral thesis. The ambient roar was devoid of honest, drunken tribalism; it was replaced by a venomous, sibilant crossfire of unemployed postdocs disputing one another’s statistical methods in furious whispers, and, beneath it all, the frantic whirring of algorithmically ill-written simulations, written hastily by a computationally inept student who misunderstood the linear algebra, running on decade-old laptops, which were bought by parents ecstatically daydreaming the Nobel Prize speech of their newly college-accepted child.

Axel slouched at the bar, choking down a synth-brew that tasted remarkably like warm urine, which was a great comfort to him, because cold urine was the only thing worse. Beside it lay a saucer of heterogamically salted peanuts that were either too salty to eat or not salty enough to taste, so that by eating them together he achieved a flawless state of total deprivation. He was drowning in thesis draft 0.165, working with the furious, dedicated inefficiency of a man who knew that doing nothing would take far too long. Magnificent academic progress was being made: he was replacing every instance of regulation with modulation. It was called the Academic Nexus, which was an extraordinarily clever name for a dingy tavern where nothing connected and nobody learned anything. The clientele consisted strictly of two groups: those who were desperately trying to climb into the ivory tower of science, and those who had been thrown out of its upper-story windows, and both groups were united in the absolute conviction that the other had made the worse mistake. Cash was entirely useless because nobody possessed any, so business was transacted in expertise—a peculiar commodity that was utterly worthless to anyone who had it and indispensable to anyone who did not. Graduate students with terminally undernourished theses and datasets that had violently Bayesploded—a tragic mathematical condition in which the evidence became so probable that it ceased to be true—could purchase salvation from disgraced Doctors of Philosophy like Morty. Morty was an intellectual of immense distinction who possessed three degrees, none of which could be eaten, and who now derived a modest living from untraceable micro-tokens, off-the-record academic fraud, and pints of a lukewarm, synthetic grease that the bartender optimistically identified as beer. It tasted like machine coolant, but Morty drank it with the serene confidence of a man who knew that so long as human stupidity remained fully accredited, he would never truly starve."
